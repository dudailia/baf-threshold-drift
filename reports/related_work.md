# Related work: threshold drift and recalibration on BAF

Search date: 2026-10-16. Sources searched: Semantic Scholar citations API for arXiv:2211.13358 (71 citing records), OpenAlex (works citing the BAF paper, title search and records), arXiv abstract pages and full-text PDFs, publisher and project pages, and general web search. Every item below was opened in this session at the URL given. Items marked "(abstract only)" were judged from the abstract; the others were also checked in the full text for the specific points stated.

No paper found in this search studies month-by-month drift of a fixed-FPR threshold on BAF, or compares recalibration strategies on BAF. The closest work is Feedzai's "Prisoners of Their Own Devices" (item 8), which shows realized FPR and the age-group FPR gap drifting away from a 5% FPR threshold over time, but on Feedzai's private account-opening data and with label noise as the cause. Two non-peer-reviewed BAF code projects (items 5 and 6) report that a fixed threshold or conformal guarantee set on an earlier month misses its target on later months, without comparing recalibration strategies.

## 1. Work that cites or uses BAF

1. *Is it Still Fair? A Comparative Evaluation of Fairness Algorithms through the Lens of Covariate Drift.* Deho, Bewong, Kwashie et al. Machine Learning (Springer), 2025 (arXiv 2024). Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.12428 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.12428. It trains 4 baseline and 7 fairness-aware algorithms on an early time period and tests them on two later periods across 5 datasets, one of which is BAF (split into "seasons" with month 0-2 as the historical period and month 6 among the later ones), and finds that fairness measured on one snapshot can reverse at a later time. It is the only peer-reviewed work found that tracks group fairness over time on BAF, but it reports equalized odds and statistical parity with weighted F1, uses no fixed-FPR operating point, does not treat threshold drift separately from model drift, and reports little covariate drift and little unfairness on BAF.

2. *Evaluating Transfer Learning Methods on Real-World Data Streams: A Case Study in Financial Fraud Detection.* Pereira, Bono, Ferreira et al. ECML PKDD, 2025. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.02702 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.02702. Feedzai authors build a framework that simulates data and label availability over time (including a fixed label delay, set to one month on BAF) and evaluates transfer learning methods month by month, reporting recall at 1% FPR. It shares our monthly, delayed-label view of BAF and uses a fixed-FPR metric, but recall at 1% FPR is computed per period as a ranking measure; the paper does not carry a threshold forward or measure realized FPR against a budget.

3. *When Can Fraud Operations Authorize Automation? A Decision-Support Framework for Fresh Audit Evidence and Review Workload.* Deng. arXiv preprint, 2026. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08577 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.08577. It decides which score regions may be automated given delayed, audited labels, evidence age and analyst capacity, and runs a BAF stress test with a simulated one-month label delay and a 5% block limit. It overlaps on delayed labels, review workload and temporal change on BAF, but its question is certification of automated actions, not the drift of a single FPR threshold or group fairness.

4. *TEMPLAR fraud: a verifier grounded calibrated and cost sensitive framework for transaction fraud detection.* Hossain, Akter, Chy et al. Discover Data, 2026. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1007/s44248-026-00117-y. It combines several model components with calibration and a validation-selected cost-sensitive threshold, evaluated on BAF Base and IEEE-CIS with chronological splits and a "temporal future-slice" test. It sets a threshold on validation and tests it later on BAF, but it reports AUROC, AUC-PR, F1 and savings rather than realized FPR against a budget, and does not compare recalibration strategies or look at age groups (abstract only).

5. *onboarding-fraud-triage* (GitHub repository, user Adnan082, portfolio project, not peer reviewed). 2026. Opened: https://github.com/Adnan082/onboarding-fraud-triage. It fits a conformal triage policy once on BAF month 5, tests on months 6 and 7, runs label-free drift monitors (PSI, a domain classifier, a conformal-rate test), and reports the age FPR ratio. It reports that the promised 1% rate of genuine applicants sent to review was not met on months 6 and 7 (1.6% and 1.3%), which is the same kind of budget miss we study; it does not recalibrate the threshold month by month or compare label-free against delayed-label recalibration.

6. *Explainable-fraud-detection-BAF* (GitHub repository, Sydney Ndabai, MSc dissertation, De Montfort University, not peer reviewed). 2026. Opened: https://github.com/s4sydney/Explainable-fraud-detection-BAF. It sets a 5% FPR threshold on month 6, tests once on month 7, and audits predictive equality across age groups (FPR 12.7% for applicants over 50 against 3.7% for younger ones). It also reports that on BAF Variant IV the realized FPR moved from 3.9% to 6.5% against the 5% target and concludes that recalibration is needed, but it evaluates a single test month and proposes no recalibration method.

## 2. Feedzai publications on BAF and fairness in fraud

7. *FairGBM: Gradient Boosting with Fairness Constraints.* Cruz, Belém, Jesus et al. ICLR, 2023. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07850 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.07850. It trains gradient boosted trees under fairness constraints and evaluates on Feedzai's account-opening fraud data (8 months) with a business rule of at most 5% FPR and predictive equality (FPR parity) across age groups. It is the source of the 5% FPR plus age-group FPR evaluation convention we use, but it evaluates on one test split and does not examine how the threshold or the FPR ratio behave over later months.

8. *Prisoners of Their Own Devices: How Models Induce Data Bias in Performative Prediction.* Pombal, Saleiro, Figueiredo et al. ICML 2022 Workshop on Responsible Decision Making in Dynamic Environments. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.13183 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2206.13183. On a real account-opening fraud case it simulates feedback-induced distribution shift and selective labels, sets a global threshold at 5% FPR on (noisy) validation data, and shows over 4 time steps that the true production FPR rises well above 5% and the gap between "Old" and "Young" groups widens, comparing a global threshold with group-wise thresholds. This is the closest prior work to our question (threshold drift at a 5% FPR budget and age-group FPR over time), but the drift is driven by simulated label noise on private data, and it does not test label-free or delayed-label recalibration.

9. *Understanding Unfairness in Fraud Detection through Model and Data Bias Interactions.* Pombal, Cruz, Bravo et al. KDD 2022 Workshop on Machine Learning in Finance. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.06273 and https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.06273. It proposes a taxonomy of data bias and tests how fairness-blind models trade off accuracy and predictive equality under each bias type on account-opening fraud data, with a global threshold at 5% FPR and the last 2 of 8 months used for testing. It shares the operating point and fairness metric, and its finding that unfairness has high variance is relevant to how we report intervals, but it does not study change over months.

10. *Promoting Fairness through Hyperparameter Optimization.* Cruz, Saleiro, Belém et al. IEEE ICDM, 2021 (extends the Fairband preprint, https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03665). Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.12715. It adds fairness objectives to random search, TPE and a bandit method, and validates on a real bank account opening fraud case (abstract only). Overlap is limited to the setting: model selection for fairness at training time, not threshold maintenance after deployment.

11. *Aequitas: A Bias and Fairness Audit Toolkit.* Saleiro, Kuester, Hinkson et al. arXiv, 2018. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05577. It is an open-source toolkit for auditing group bias and fairness metrics across subgroups (abstract only). It provides the group-ratio style of fairness audit (such as FPR disparity) that our FPR ratio follows, but has no temporal or threshold component.

12. *Unprocessing Seven Years of Algorithmic Fairness.* Cruz, Hardt. ICLR, 2024. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.07261. It shows on tabular data that postprocessing (group-specific thresholds) reaches a fairness-accuracy frontier containing the other methods it could evaluate. It supports group-specific thresholding as a strong baseline, which matters if a single recalibrated threshold keeps the budget but not the FPR ratio; it does not address drift over time (abstract only).

## 3. Threshold recalibration and prior or label shift adjustment

13. *Adjusting the Outputs of a Classifier to New a Priori Probabilities: A Simple Procedure.* Saerens, Latinne, Decaestecker. Neural Computation, 2002. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W2141253686. It gives an EM procedure that re-estimates class priors on unlabeled new data and adjusts posterior outputs without refitting the model (abstract only). It is the classical label-free correction for prevalence change; under pure prior shift, an FPR-targeted threshold should stay put while alert volume changes, which is a useful reference point for separating prevalence change from score-distribution change on BAF.

14. *Detecting and Correcting for Label Shift with Black Box Predictors.* Lipton, Wang, Smola. ICML, 2018. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916. BBSE estimates the new label marginal from a black-box predictor's confusion matrix and unlabeled target data, then corrects the classifier (abstract only). It offers a label-free way to estimate monthly fraud prevalence, which could separate a label-free quantile threshold's error into prevalence and shape components, but it assumes p(x|y) is fixed, which BAF's monthly drift may violate.

15. *Test-time Recalibration of Conformal Predictors Under Distribution Shift Based on Unlabeled Examples.* Yilmaz, Heckel. arXiv, 2022. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.04166. It predicts a new cutoff threshold for a shifted distribution from unlabeled examples (Quantile Thresholded Confidence), and states that no unlabeled method can guarantee validity in general (abstract only). It is a direct analogue of our label-free quantile recalibration, applied to image classifiers and conformal sets rather than a fraud FPR budget, and its impossibility remark frames what label-free recalibration can and cannot promise.

16. *Neyman-Pearson (NP) classification algorithms and NP receiver operating characteristics (NP-ROC).* Tong, Feng, Li. Science Advances, 2018. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.03109. It gives an umbrella algorithm that picks a score threshold so that type I error (FPR) stays below a target with high probability, and notes that matching the empirical FPR to the target does not control the true FPR. It is the finite-sample version of "hit 5% FPR on validation" and implies that a threshold matching 5% FPR on a validation sample can exceed 5% on new data even without drift, which our budget-miss baseline should account for; it assumes no distribution shift.

17. *Decoupling Decision-Making in Fraud Prevention through Classifier Calibration for Business Logic Action.* Luzio, Ponti, Arevalo et al. ACM SAC, 2024. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.05240. It uses calibration methods to decouple score-based business rules from model updates in fraud prevention and finds isotonic and beta calibration best when training and test data are shifted (abstract only). It addresses keeping score-based actions stable as models and data change, but through probability calibration rather than threshold recalibration to a fixed FPR, and without a monthly budget analysis.

## 4. Fixed-FPR or fixed alert-budget operation in fraud detection

18. *Credit Card Fraud Detection: A Realistic Modeling and a Novel Learning Strategy.* Dal Pozzolo, Boracchi, Caelen et al. IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, 2017 (OpenAlex year). Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W2756359217. It formalizes the operating conditions of a production fraud system (few alerts checked by investigators, verification latency, concept drift) and argues for measures tied to the alerts investigators can review (abstract only). It establishes limited investigator capacity as the operating constraint in fraud, but the abstract describes no threshold carried forward across periods and no measurement of a realized alert or FPR rate against a target.

19. *Imbalanced Classification under Capacity Constraints.* Fraiman, Fraiman. arXiv, 2026. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03289. It defines the classifier that maximizes minority-class sensitivity subject to a bound on the share of cases flagged, shows it reduces to reweighted Bayes or post-hoc thresholding, and says the framework extends to online settings (abstract only). It formalizes the alert-budget constraint we impose, but its experiments use a static credit-default dataset and do not examine budget drift over time.

20. *Cost-Sensitive Learning to Defer to Multiple Experts with Workload Constraints.* Alves, Leitão, Jesus et al. Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2024. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.06906. Feedzai authors assign fraud cases between a model and synthetic analysts under per-analyst capacity limits, minimizing misclassification cost (abstract only). It treats review capacity as a hard constraint, as we do, but assumes capacity is met by assignment within a batch, not by a threshold whose alert volume drifts across months.

21. *Dynamic Threshold Calibration for Financial Fraud Detection Using Temporal Graph Neural Networks: A Support-Aware Bounded Approach.* Thornton, Redford. International Journal of Data Sciences and Intelligent Systems, 2026. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W7220829996. On the Elliptic Bitcoin graph it updates the threshold at each time step from the most recent delayed labels, damps the update when positives are scarce and caps threshold movement, and compares this with a frozen validation threshold and rolling thresholding, with a label-delay stress test showing the benefit shrinks as labels get older (abstract only). This is the closest methodological match to our delayed-label strategy and lag sweep, but it targets F1 rather than an FPR or alert budget, uses different data, and has no fairness analysis.

22. *Dynamic Calibration of Decision Thresholds for Financial Anomaly Detection.* Huang, Wang, Zhou et al. Journal of Global Information Management, 2025. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W7116940237. It replaces a fixed Isolation Forest anomaly-score threshold with one updated online from the evolving score distribution, motivated by deterioration of fixed thresholds under traffic shift (abstract only). It is a label-free, score-distribution-based threshold update like our quantile strategy, but for unsupervised detection and without an FPR target or group metrics.

23. *Who the Screen Flags: Auditing and Mitigating Structure-Conditioned False-Positive Burden in Anti-Money-Laundering Screening.* Paul. IEEE Access, 2026. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1109/ACCESS.2026.3724786. On the synthetic AMLworld benchmark it fits thresholds on validation at fixed alert budgets (5%, 10%, 20%), applies them unchanged to a temporally disjoint test set, and audits group false-positive burden with clustered bootstrap intervals, finding the disparity estimates fragile and not transferable across budgets (abstract only). It is close in design (validation-fitted threshold, fixed budget, group FP disparity, careful intervals), but uses one test period, groups defined by network structure rather than age, and does not study recalibration.

## 5. Delayed labels and verification latency

24. *Credit card fraud detection and concept-drift adaptation with delayed supervised information.* Dal Pozzolo, Boracchi, Caelen et al. IJCNN, 2015. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W1660289459. It separates fast investigator feedback on alerts from delayed labels for the rest of the transactions and shows that training separate models on each and aggregating them improves alert precision (abstract only). It establishes that fraud labels arrive late and in two streams, which motivates our 1 to 3 month lag; it adapts the model, not the threshold.

25. *Change with Delayed Labeling: When is it Detectable?* Žliobaitė. IEEE ICDM Workshops, 2010. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W2157664934. It analyses which kinds of distribution change can be detected without labels by monitoring the stream of classifier outputs, and which cannot (abstract only). It gives the theoretical reason a label-free quantile strategy can track some drift (changes in the score distribution) but must miss changes that only alter p(y|x), which bounds what our label-free strategy can achieve on TPR.

26. *Data Stream Classification Guided by Clustering on Nonstationary Environments and Extreme Verification Latency.* Souza, Silva, Gama et al. SIAM SDM, 2015. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W2252617635. It proposes SCARGC, which adapts a stream classifier by clustering when true labels never arrive (abstract only). It represents the "no labels ever" end of the latency range, the regime of our label-free strategy, but adapts class boundaries rather than an operating threshold under a budget.

27. *Delayed labelling evaluation for data streams.* Grzenda, Gomes, Bifet. Data Mining and Knowledge Discovery, 2019. Opened: https://api.openalex.org/works/W2989114215. It proposes continuous re-evaluation for stream classifiers when labels arrive with latency and shows that ignoring delay misstates performance (abstract only). It supports our rule that a delayed-label strategy may use only labels that would exist at deployment time; it concerns evaluation protocol, not threshold choice.

## 6. Group fairness over time and under distribution shift

28. *A Survey on Preserving Fairness Guarantees in Changing Environments.* Barrainkua, Gordaliza, Lozano et al. arXiv, 2022 (submitted to ACM Computing Surveys). Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.07530. It surveys fair classification under distribution shift between training and deployment and proposes a taxonomy of approaches and benchmarks (abstract only). It confirms that loss of fairness guarantees under shift is a recognized research line; it is a survey, with no fraud or fixed-FPR results.

29. *Ensuring Fairness under Prior Probability Shifts.* Biswas, Mukherjee. arXiv, 2020. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.03474. It shows that prior probability shifts between yearly records can make a classifier unfair to subgroups and proposes CAPE, which uses prevalence estimation to keep a shift-aware fairness notion (abstract only). It is relevant because BAF fraud prevalence changes by month and may change differently by age group, which would move group FPRs even under a recalibrated global threshold; it uses COMPAS and MEPS, not fraud.

30. *Empirical observation of negligible fairness-accuracy trade-offs in machine learning for public policy.* Rodolfa, Lamba, Ghani. Nature Machine Intelligence, 2021. Opened: https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.02972. In resource-constrained allocation (a fixed number of interventions, analogous to an alert budget) it applies post-hoc group-specific threshold adjustments and finds fairness improves at little accuracy cost, a result it reports holding over time (abstract only). It is the closest evidence that budget-constrained group thresholds can keep fairness across time periods, but in public-policy settings rather than fraud and not with an FPR budget.

## Verdict

Already answered by existing work: that a threshold fixed on one period stops meeting its rate target later, in general and in fraud (items 5, 6, 8, 21, 22); that group FPR gaps can widen over time under a fixed 5% FPR threshold (item 8, on private data with simulated label noise); that fairness on BAF measured at one time can change at a later time (item 1, with equalized odds and statistical parity, not FPR parity at a fixed budget); that label-free threshold or quantile re-estimation is possible but cannot be guaranteed in general (items 13 to 15, 25); that delayed-label threshold updating helps less as labels get older (item 21, on Elliptic with an F1 target); and that 5% FPR with age-group predictive equality is the standard BAF operating point (items 7, 9).

Still open, as far as this search found: no work measures, month by month on BAF, how realized FPR, TPR and alert volume drift after a threshold is set once on a validation month at 5% FPR. No work tracks the age-group FPR ratio across BAF months under a fixed threshold. No work compares static, label-free monthly quantile, delayed-label (1 to 3 month lag) and Oracle (upper bound, not deployable) thresholds on BAF, or on any public fraud dataset, by budget miss and detection loss. No work tests whether a recalibration that restores the aggregate 5% FPR also restores, or worsens, the FPR ratio between age groups. The two BAF code projects that come closest (items 5 and 6) are not peer reviewed, test only one or two months, and compare no recalibration strategies. These parts of the project question are open; the general phenomena are known, so the contribution would be the BAF-specific measurement and the head-to-head comparison, not the observation that thresholds drift.
